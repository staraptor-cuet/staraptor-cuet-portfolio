## The five stresses an airframe must carry

The FAA Aviation Maintenance Technician Handbook (Airframe) says every aircraft part is designed to carry a load. External loads cause stress. Stress is the material's internal resistance to being deformed. Strain is how much the material actually deforms. Any load deforms a material a little, however strong the material is. The handbook lists five major stresses:

- **Tension:** a force that pulls a part apart. Tensile strength is the load needed to break the part divided by its cross-sectional area.
- **Compression:** a force that squeezes or shortens a part.
- **Torsion:** a force that twists a part.
- **Shear:** a force that makes one layer of material slide over the next. The shear strength of a material is either equal to or less than its tensile or compressive strength.
- **Bending:** compression and tension acting together. The inside of the bend is compressed and the outside is stretched.

One structural part can carry several of these stresses at once. The handbook says most structural members are designed to carry end loads, meaning tension or compression along their length, rather than side loads that bend them. Some parts, such as cowlings and fairings, carry little load. Their main job is a smooth shape that reduces drag.

## Fuselage construction: truss, monocoque and semi-monocoque

The FAA Airframe handbook calls the fuselage the main body of a fixed-wing aircraft. The fuselage holds the equipment, and on single-engine aircraft it holds the powerplant. The handbook describes two general types of fuselage construction.

- **Truss:** a rigid framework of beams, struts and bars that resists deformation. A truss fuselage is often welded steel tubing, and each member can carry tension and compression. Small aircraft may use bolted or riveted aluminium trusses braced with rods or tubes.
- **Monocoque ("single shell"):** the skin carries the main loads. Formers, frame assemblies and bulkheads give the shape. The heaviest of these sit where concentrated loads enter, such as the wing, engine and stabiliser attachments. The main problem with monocoque construction is keeping enough strength without too much weight.
- **Semi-monocoque:** monocoque construction with the skin reinforced by longitudinal members. Longerons are strong members that run across several frames and help the skin carry bending loads. Stringers are lighter and more numerous. They give the shape and hold the skin in place. Together, stringers and longerons stop tension and compression from bending the fuselage.

The handbook says most modern aircraft use semi-monocoque construction. Because the loads are shared between the skin and many members, no single piece is failure-critical. A semi-monocoque fuselage can therefore take considerable damage and still hold together. A gusset is a connecting bracket that adds strength at a joint.

## Wing structure: spars, ribs, skin and stringers

The FAA Airframe handbook describes the inside of most wings as spars and stringers running spanwise (root to tip), plus ribs and formers running chordwise (leading edge to trailing edge).

- **Spars** are the main structural members of the wing. They carry all the distributed loads and the concentrated weights, such as the fuselage, landing gear and engines. The handbook calls the spar the most important part of the wing, because most of the stress from the other members is passed to it. A wing usually has two spars. One sits near the front and the other about two-thirds of the way back toward the trailing edge.
- **False spars** do not run the full span. They are often used to hold control-surface hinges, such as an aileron spar.
- **Ribs** give the wing its cambered shape and pass loads from the skin and stringers to the spars.
- **Skin** carries part of the flight loads and passes stress to the ribs.

A **cantilever** wing needs no external bracing. A **semi-cantilever** wing uses struts or wires. The handbook names three basic wing layouts. A monospar wing has one main spanwise member. A multispar wing has more than one. A box beam has two main spars joined by bulkheads. The handbook calls it fail-safe design when, if one member of a structure fails, another part takes over its load. One example is a spar made in two sections.

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## What a composite is and why fibre direction matters

The FAA Airframe handbook defines an advanced composite as a fibre material set in a resin matrix. The plies are usually laid with fibres in alternating directions to give strength and stiffness. The main advantages it lists are high strength, relatively low weight and corrosion resistance.

- The **fibre** carries the load. A composite is strong and stiff only in the direction of its fibres. A material whose properties change with direction is called **anisotropic**. Metals such as aluminium are **isotropic**, which means they have the same properties in every direction.
- The **matrix** (the resin) holds the fibres in position, passes loads to them, protects them from the environment, and sets the highest temperature the part can work at.

The handbook says the order and angle of the plies (the **stacking sequence**) control stiffness and strength. A part might need 0° plies for axial loads, ±45° plies for shear loads and 90° plies for side loads. A **quasi-isotropic** layup stacks plies at 0°, −45°, 45° and 90°, or at 0°, −60° and 60°, so the laminate behaves more like a metal. Unidirectional tape has strength only along its fibres. Woven fabric is bidirectional, with fibres usually 90° apart. Carbon roving is sold as 3K, 6K or 12K, where K means 1,000 filaments. The **warp** is the lengthwise, higher-strength direction of a fabric.

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## Carbon fibre, glass fibre and aramid compared

The FAA Airframe handbook compares the main fibre types this way:

| Fibre | What the handbook says |
|---|---|
| Fibreglass (E-glass, S-glass) | Lower cost. Resists chemical and galvanic corrosion. Does not conduct electricity. Used for secondary structure such as fairings, radomes and wing tips. S-glass and S2-glass are stronger than E-glass. |
| Aramid (Kevlar) | Light, strong and tough, with high resistance to impact damage. Weak in compression and absorbs water: some parts took up to 8% of their weight. Hard to drill and cut. Natural colour is yellow. |
| Carbon/graphite | 3 to 10 times stiffer than glass fibre. Used for primary structure. High cost. Conducts electricity less well than aluminium, so parts prone to lightning strikes need a protective mesh or coating. High risk of galvanic corrosion when used with metal fasteners and structures. |

The handbook also says the useful strength and stiffness of carbon fibre ranges from about the level of fibreglass up to the level of titanium, depending on how the plies are oriented to the load. **Galvanic corrosion** is corrosion that happens when two electrically different materials touch in the presence of moisture.

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## Resins and how they cure

The FAA Airframe handbook says epoxies are polymerizable thermosetting resins. Thermosetting means they harden through a chemical reaction. Epoxies are widely used in prepregs and structural adhesives. The handbook lists their advantages as high strength and stiffness, low volatiles, excellent adhesion, low shrinkage, good chemical resistance and easy processing. Their main drawbacks are brittleness and a loss of properties when moisture is present. Curing temperatures range from room temperature to about 180 °C (350 °F). The most common range is 120–180 °C (250–350 °F).

The handbook describes three cure stages:

- **A stage:** resin and hardener are mixed, but the reaction has not started. A wet lay-up uses resin in this stage.
- **B stage:** the reaction has started and the resin is thick and tacky. Prepreg resin is kept in the B stage by storing it in a freezer at 0 °F (about −18 °C). Curing restarts when the material is taken out and warms up.
- **C stage:** the resin is fully cured.

**Prepreg** is fibre that comes already impregnated with B-stage resin, in tape or fabric form. The handbook says room-temperature wet lay-up is easy and its materials store well. However, room-temperature wet lay-up does not restore the strength and durability of parts originally cured at 250 °F (about 120 °C) or 350 °F (about 180 °C). In general, wet lay-up properties are lower than prepreg properties.

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## Wet lay-up and vacuum bagging in practice

Easy Composites, a composite materials supplier, publishes a tutorial on hand-laminating a carbon part in a mould and then vacuum bagging it:

1. Coat the mould with release wax. The tutorial uses five coats, 10–15 minutes apart, each buffed to a shine.
2. Brush epoxy onto the mould. Press dry cloth into the wet resin, then add resin and the next layer. The example uses six layers of 210 g carbon cloth, each about 0.25 mm thick, for about 1.5 mm in total. The pot life of the resin used is about 10 minutes.
3. Lay peel ply on the laminate, then breather cloth. Seal the bag to the mould with sealant tape and fit a connector.
4. Use about 20% vacuum. A vacuum near 100% pulls too much resin out of the laminate. Keep the pump running until the initial cure, about 6 hours with the resin and fast hardener in the example.

The tutorial says vacuum bagging reduces the risk of air voids, removes excess resin and improves the surface finish. A fully cured part should give a sharp ringing sound when tapped. A dull sound means the resin is not fully cured yet.

The FAA Airframe handbook explains the bagging layers. **Peel ply** is torn off later to leave a clean surface for bonding. **Perforated release film** lets air out and stops the bleeder from sticking. The **bleeder** soaks up excess resin. The **breather** keeps an air path open to the vacuum port.

Source: https://www.easycomposites.co.uk/learning/hand-laminate-vacuum-bag-simple-part ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## Sandwich panels and core materials

The FAA Airframe handbook says sandwich construction gives high bending stiffness at minimal weight. A sandwich has thin face sheets bonded to a light core. Increasing the core thickness greatly increases stiffness while adding little weight. A figure in the handbook sets a solid laminate at 1.0 for stiffness, flexural strength and weight, then shows two sandwich panels with progressively thicker cores:

| Panel | Stiffness | Flexural strength | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid laminate | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| Sandwich, thinner core | 7.0 | 3.5 | 1.03 |
| Sandwich, thicker core | 37.0 | 9.2 | 1.06 |

**Core materials** named by the handbook:

- **Honeycomb:** usually hexagonal cells. Aramid paper (Nomex or Korex) is the most common aircraft core. Smaller cells support face sheets better. Higher-density core is stronger and stiffer.
- **Foam:** used on homebuilt and light aircraft for wing tips, flight controls, fuselage sections, wings and wing ribs. Foam is typically heavier and weaker than honeycomb. Aircraft-grade polystyrene and polypropylene can be cut with a hot wire. Polyurethane must not be hot-wire cut, and polypropylene dissolves in fuels and solvents. PVC foams (such as Divinycell and Airex) and polymethacrylimide (Rohacell) are closed-cell foams.
- **Balsa:** a natural wood with elongated closed cells. Balsa is less than half the density of ordinary wood but denser than other structural cores.

Face sheets can be very thin, sometimes only 3 or 4 plies, and field reports say these have poor impact resistance. Carbon face sheets cannot be used on aluminium honeycomb, because the aluminium corrodes.

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## Carbon tubes and rods for spars and booms

Easy Composites explains the main ways carbon tubes are made:

- **Pultruded:** fibres are pulled through a resin bath and a heated die, so every fibre runs along the tube. This gives maximum strength along the length, but the tube is more prone to crushing, splitting and twisting failure.
- **Roll-wrapped:** prepreg layers are wrapped around a mandrel and oven-cured. Some layers run around the tube (hoop fibres), which improves crush and split resistance at a small cost in stiffness. A woven outer layer improves hoop strength and reduces splintering.
- **Filament-wound:** fibres are wound mainly for hoop strength, as in pressure vessels.

The supplier tested tubes of 10 mm outer diameter and 8 mm inner diameter. In three-point bending, roll-wrapped carbon held about 200 kg, stainless steel about 175 kg, pultruded carbon about 140 kg and aluminium about 50 kg. In torsion, stainless steel did best and aluminium came second. Both carbon tubes did poorly, with roll-wrapped slightly better than pultruded. Stainless steel (8 g/cm³) is nearly 5.5 times as dense as carbon, and aluminium (2.7 g/cm³) nearly twice as dense. Once weight is taken into account, carbon came out well ahead in every test except torsion.

The same page says carbon severely deteriorates above 120 °C, is not suited to high abrasion such as sliding bearings, and holds cut threads poorly. It recommends metal inserts for threads and bonded inserts or sleeves at joints. The FAA handbook adds that ±45° plies carry shear loads, and torsion is a shear load.

Source: https://www.easycomposites.eu/learning/carbon-fibre-tubes-explained ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## 3D-printed parts: layers, walls and anisotropy

The Prusa knowledge base says a part made by FFF (fused filament fabrication, also called FDM) is "stronger in one direction than another," like wood. Structural parts should therefore be oriented to suit their loads. A large model can be split into pieces, each piece printed in its best orientation, and the pieces glued together. Pegs help with alignment and strength.

Prusa also says a printed part's strength comes mostly from the number of **perimeters** (outer walls), not from the infill. To make a part stronger, add perimeters. Prusa profiles use at least 2 perimeters. Prusa suggests at least 3 top layers. The count depends on layer height: 3 top layers at 0.3 mm layer height and 9 top layers at 0.1 mm.

Prusament datasheets show how much weaker the bond between layers is than the filament itself:

| Material | Filament tensile yield strength | Interlayer adhesion |
|---|---|---|
| Prusament PLA | 57 MPa | 17 MPa |
| Prusament PETG V0 (flame-retardant grade) | 39.4 MPa | 16.4 MPa |
| Prusament ASA | 40 MPa | 11 MPa |

**MPa** (megapascal) is a unit of stress, equal to one newton per square millimetre. The printed test specimens in these datasheets used 0.20 mm layers, 2 perimeters and 100% infill.

Source: https://help.prusa3d.com/article/modeling-with-3d-printing-in-mind_164135 ; https://help.prusa3d.com/article/layers-and-perimeters_1748 ; https://prusament.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/PLA_Prusament_TDS_2021_10_EN.pdf ; https://prusament.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/PETG_V0_ENG.pdf ; https://prusament.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/ASA_Prusament_TDS_2022_16_EN.pdf

## Heat softening and choosing filaments for hot parts

Printed plastics soften when they get warm. Datasheets describe this in two ways. The **glass transition temperature** is where the plastic starts to turn from hard to rubbery. The **heat deflection temperature (HDT)** is the temperature at which a test bar bends by a set amount under a set load. The Prusament datasheets give HDT measured to the standard ISO 75 at 0.45 MPa and 1.80 MPa.

| Material | Stated value |
|---|---|
| colorFabb LW-PLA | Glass transition 55 °C |
| Prusament PLA | HDT 55 °C (both loads) |
| Prusament PETG V0 | HDT 68 °C (0.45 MPa), 74 °C (1.80 MPa) |
| Prusament ASA | HDT 93 °C (0.45 MPa), 86 °C (1.80 MPa) |

A PLA or LW-PLA part used close to 55 °C may therefore lose stiffness. Parts near warm motors or electronics, or parts left in strong sun, may need a material with a higher HDT, such as PETG or ASA. Their recommended print settings are also hotter. The Prusament datasheets list a 230 ± 10 °C nozzle and 80 ± 10 °C bed for PETG V0, and a 260 ± 10 °C nozzle and 110 ± 5 °C bed for ASA. The densities are 1.24 g/cm³ for PLA, 1.27 g/cm³ for PETG V0 and 1.07 g/cm³ for ASA.

Source: https://colorfabb.com/lw-pla-natural ; https://prusament.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/PLA_Prusament_TDS_2021_10_EN.pdf ; https://prusament.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/PETG_V0_ENG.pdf ; https://prusament.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/ASA_Prusament_TDS_2022_16_EN.pdf

## How foaming LW-PLA settings work

colorFabb's LW-PLA contains an active foaming agent. Its product page gives these figures:

- Foaming starts at about 230 °C. The material can grow to nearly 3 times its unfoamed volume.
- Because the plastic expands, the material multiplier (flow) is set about 3 times lower than for regular PLA.
- Unfoamed density is 1.21–1.43 g/cm³. Fully foamed density is 0.403–0.476 g/cm³. colorFabb says this reduces the weight of printed parts by up to 65% compared with regular PLA.
- Stated settings: nozzle 195–260 °C, print speed 40–100 mm/s, heated bed 50–60 °C (not strictly necessary).
- Glass transition temperature is 55 °C.

The logic behind the settings is that temperature and flow work together. Foaming starts at about 230 °C, inside the stated nozzle range, and the expansion is why the flow is set lower. The colorFabb page does not give a density for each temperature, so each printer has to be calibrated with test prints.

Source: https://colorfabb.com/lw-pla-natural

## Wood construction and adhesives

Model Aviation, the monthly magazine of the US Academy of Model Aeronautics, describes the common adhesives used in balsa and light plywood models:

- **Epoxy:** a two-part glue. All epoxies are non-shrinking and make very rigid bonds. The bond is usually much stronger than the balsa or light plywood it joins. Five-minute epoxy hardens fast but is less than ideal for long-term use because of its brittleness. The article names a slower epoxy with an 8–12 minute working time, ready for use in 30–35 minutes. Epoxy is heavy, so the article advises against using it for general construction.
- **CA (cyanoacrylate, "instant glue"):** comes in thin, medium and thick grades. Thin CA cures in about 1–3 seconds and medium in about 5–10 seconds. CA is relatively brittle and its fumes can be very irritating. It can be debonded with CA debonder or acetone.
- **Aliphatic (yellow) wood glue:** described as ideal for general balsa construction. It is non-toxic and sandable when dry, and takes about 24 hours to reach full strength.

The FAA Airframe handbook says wood wing ribs are usually made of spruce, in plywood-web, lightened plywood-web or truss types. Of these, the truss type is the most efficient but the most complex to build.

Source: https://www.modelaviation.com/article/glues-free-flight ; https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## Mounting the flight controller against vibration

The ArduPilot vibration-damping guide explains how to isolate the autopilot, the flight-control computer, from frame vibration:

- **Foam:** 1–2 cm squares of foam, one under each corner of the autopilot.
- **Gel pads:** 1–2 cm squares at each corner, with any strap not so tight that it stops the pads damping. The guide says Moon Gel has been shown to fail in heat above 100 °F (about 38 °C).
- **O-ring suspension:** a platform hung on 1/16-inch nylon O-rings at each corner, generally 1/2 to 3/4 inch outer diameter. Silicone O-rings generally damp better than Buna-N rubber.
- Sorbothane, in the guide's experience, becomes permanently compressed and damps less well than gel.

The guide also addresses the sources of vibration. Balance propellers and, where needed, motors. Use straight prop adapters and motors with healthy bearings. Make frame arms as rigid as possible. Aluminium or carbon arms twist and bend less, which reduces vibration. The guide reports example setups reaching about 0.05 G, and one mount improved to 0.02 G. ("G" here is acceleration measured in units of gravity.)

Source: https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/common-vibration-damping.html

## Weight control and centre of gravity during the build

The ArduPilot Plane guide says the centre of gravity (CG), the aircraft's balance point, is critical to good flight. As a rough guide, it sits about one-third of the way back from the front of the wing, measured along the chord (the front-to-back width of the wing). The guide's advice:

- Check the CG figure in a model's instructions against other sources, such as forums or pilots who have flown the model, because cheaper models fairly often list incorrect values.
- Adjust balance by moving the battery or by adding or removing weight.
- If unsure, make the aircraft slightly nose-heavy. A nose-heavy aircraft may have trouble taking off and climbing but is usually stable. A tail-heavy aircraft may be very unstable and can crash shortly after takeoff.
- After the first flight, look at the pitch integrator (PIDP.I) in the log. A value consistently above zero means the aircraft needed up-elevator, which suggests it is slightly nose-heavy or has wrong trim. A value consistently below zero suggests it is slightly tail-heavy or has wrong trim.

During manufacture, material choices change weight. One example is the up-to-65% weight reduction colorFabb gives for foamed LW-PLA compared with regular PLA. Another is Model Aviation's note that epoxy is heavy. A builder therefore needs to check balance again after building, not only on paper.

Source: https://ardupilot.org/plane/docs/guide-center-of-gravity.html ; https://colorfabb.com/lw-pla-natural ; https://www.modelaviation.com/article/glues-free-flight

## Inspecting composite parts for damage

The FAA Airframe handbook lists common manufacturing defects in composites: delamination, resin-starved areas, resin-rich areas, blisters and air bubbles, wrinkles, voids and heat damage. Prepreg backing paper or separation film accidentally left between plies is another source of flaws.

- **Delamination** is a separation between laminate layers. It can grow from matrix cracks or from a low-energy impact. Delaminations have little effect when a laminate is loaded in tension. Under compression or shear, the separated layers can buckle and lead to structural failure.
- **Visual inspection** is the primary in-service method. Look for dents, scorching, stains, chips, discoloration, blisters and disbonds, using a flashlight, magnifier or mirror. A visual check cannot find internal delaminations or disbonds.
- **Tap testing (coin tapping):** tap the area with a small disk or hammer-like tool and listen. A clear, sharp, ringing sound means a well-bonded solid structure. A dull or thud-like sound means a possible defect. Tap testing is effective on thin skin bondlines and honeycomb panels with thin face sheets. Internal features that are part of the design can also change the sound.

For repairs, the handbook says to check the repair manual for the material and number of plies. Typically one more ply than the original is installed, and repair plies must have the same orientation as the original plies.

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/amt_airframe_hb_vol_1.pdf

## Workshop safety with composites and LiPo batteries

The FAA Airframe handbook gives these shop safety rules for composite work:

- Wear safety glasses at all times. Wear a face shield when mixing or pouring acids. Do not wear contact lenses in the shop, even with safety glasses, because some solvents can melt them and dust can get under them.
- Do not breathe carbon-fibre dust. Keep good airflow and use a vacuum near the dust source. A downdraft table collects sanding and grinding dust.
- Wear a properly fitted dust mask or respirator when sanding. For painting, use a sealed respirator with the correct filters or a fresh-air supply.

The University of Maryland drone lab's battery rules for LiPo (lithium-polymer) packs:

- Charge only inside a fire-resistant container, and check that the charger has detected the battery correctly.
- Do not overcharge or excessively discharge packs.
- Do not use damaged or bulging packs, and dispose of them promptly. Keep packs away from sharp objects that could puncture them.
- If a pack will sit unused for more than 14 days, put it at storage charge of 3.7–3.8 V per cell.
- If something goes wrong during charging and it is safe to act, remove the pack using heat-protective gloves and safety glasses. Do not poke or disturb the battery. Report every incident to the lab manager.
